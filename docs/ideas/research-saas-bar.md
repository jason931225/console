Status: RESEARCH — sourced, confidence-labelled

Researched 2026-07-29. External research only — no repo was read, nothing here is a claim about our system.

## How to read this

**Confidence labels**

- `CONFIRMED` — I fetched a primary source (vendor doc, legal page, regulator, standard) and it says this.
- `LIKELY` — multiple credible secondary sources agree, or a primary source says it but I only saw it via search summary rather than fetching the page.
- `UNCERTAIN` — one source, or the only sources are SEO/marketing content with unverifiable numbers.
- `UNKNOWN` — I could not establish this. Stated as a gap, not smoothed over.

**Known weakness in this research.** The search layer surfaced a large volume of 2026-dated SEO content ("Enterprise SaaS SLA Benchmark 2026", "SaaS Pricing Trends 2026") that publishes precise-sounding statistics with no traceable methodology. I have deliberately labelled every such number `UNCERTAIN` and preferred structural facts I could verify against vendor documentation. Where a plan-gating fact matters (which tier gets SSO, which tier gets audit logs), I fetched the vendor's own pricing page. Treat `UNCERTAIN` percentages as directional vibes, not evidence.

**Second known weakness.** Contract-level facts — what a real enterprise MSA actually commits to for uptime, indemnity, breach windows, or price uplift caps — are mostly not public. Published SLA pages are the *floor*, negotiated addenda are the real bar, and I cannot see those. Several rows below are floors, not norms.

## Relationship to the benchmark corpus

`docs/program/benchmark-matrix/` already compares named vendors capability-by-capability across 14 modules and 5 lenses. That corpus asks *"which vendor has feature X"*. This document asks *"what does a serious business buyer now expect as table stakes, and how is it packaged and priced"* — a different question, so most of it stands independently. Three areas overlap, and where they do I mark the row `EXTENDS <file>:<line>` and state only what I add rather than restating the corpus finding.

The recurring shape of my addition is **packaging**: the corpus asks whether a capability exists in a product; I found that the buyer-visible question is increasingly *which tier it sits in and what it costs to reach*. That is a distinct axis from feature presence, and it is where buyers report getting burned.

## 1. Trust and operations

What the business gets: a written availability number with money attached, a status page it did not have to ask for, an audit report it can hand to its own auditor, a choice about which country its data sits in, and a contractual clock on breach notification. The 2026 shift is that *transparency artefacts* have become self-service — buyers expect to complete 80% of a security review by reading a trust centre before talking to a human.

The leaders differentiate on three things: (a) tiered SLAs where the higher tier buys a genuinely different number rather than the same number with a nicer PDF, (b) data residency in the specific country asked for rather than a vague "EU", and (c) customer-managed keys where revoking the key actually stops the vendor reading the data — with the operational consequences documented honestly.

Note the asymmetry worth copying: Atlassian's SLA covers Premium and Enterprise only. Standard and Free get *no* uptime commitment. That is the common industry shape — an SLA is a paid product, not a property of the service.

## 2. Identity and access

What the business gets: employees log in with the company's identity provider, joiners/leavers are provisioned and de-provisioned automatically, phishing-resistant authentication is available, and the permission model can express the org's real shape.

The live 2026 story is authentication *strength*, not authentication *protocol*. Microsoft has committed to making passkeys the default in Entra ID: from 2026-09-01 users on SMS/voice MFA are auto-enabled and nudged into passkey registration; on 2027-02-01 Microsoft-provided SMS and voice authentication ends; after that, SMS/voice users must register a passkey before signing in. Organisations that still need SMS can bring a telecom provider via Microsoft Security Store from 2026-10-30 and pay for it. That is the largest workforce IdP telling the market that phone-based MFA is over — and it drags every downstream SaaS app into supporting phishing-resistant sign-in.

The unresolved fight is the SSO tax. ssotax.org is still actively maintained (most recent update 2026-04-24) and the markups it lists are not marginal. Meanwhile the actual gating pattern among well-regarded modern tools is *split*: SAML sometimes lands mid-tier, but SCIM and audit logs almost always sit in Enterprise.

**EXTENDS `policy.md:41,43,63,69,75,83,89,95,101,141`.** The corpus already establishes the authorization-model axis: Cedar/ABAC and Palantir's attribute-and-column policies versus SAP's rule-pair SoD versus permission-bundle RBAC in Slack/Teams/Asana/n8n (`:41,43`), enforcement grain (`:63`), scoping (`:69`), access-request routing (`:75`), the SoD-ruleset gap (`:83`), decision logging (`:89`), recertification (`:95`), attribute-driven auto-provisioning from HR events (`:101`), and just-in-time time-bound grants as a steal from Entra PIM (`:141`). I do not restate any of that.

Four things I add that the corpus does not cover:

1. **Authentication strength, with a dated industry deadline.** The corpus treats Entra as an RBAC/PIM/Access-Reviews reference. It does not cover the credential itself. Microsoft has now committed to passkeys as the Entra ID default with hard dates — this changes what every downstream app must accept, independent of any authorization model.
2. **Identity as a packaging decision.** The corpus compares authorization *models*; buyers argue about *tiers*. Which plan gets SAML, which gets SCIM, and what the step-up costs is the live commercial fight (ssotax.org), and it is invisible in a capability matrix.
3. **SCIM as distinct from HRIS-driven provisioning.** `policy.md:101` covers Rippling's attribute-cascade — permissions recomputed from HR events inside one product. That is a different mechanism from SCIM, where the *customer's IdP* is the source of truth and pushes joiners/leavers in. A buyer's IT team asks for the second; the corpus only benchmarks the first. Both are needed and they are not substitutes.
4. **Relationship-based authorization (ReBAC) as a third model.** The corpus's grammar axis is RBAC → ABAC/PBAC (`:41`). Zanzibar-derived engines (OpenFGA, SpiceDB, Permify) sit outside that axis: permissions derive from a graph of typed relationships rather than from attribute predicates, which is what makes list-filtering and inherited/nested sharing tractable. Relevant because attribute predicates lowered to SQL and relationship graphs have genuinely different performance characteristics for "which rows may this principal see".

**Table stakes vs upsell, as of 2026:** SAML/OIDC login is table stakes even in mid-market and increasingly *is* being priced into mid-tiers. SCIM is still overwhelmingly an Enterprise-tier feature — that is the current, live line. Audit logs sit with SCIM behind the same line. Passkey support is moving from differentiator to expectation on Microsoft's timetable, not the market's.

**Where buyers get burned:** buying a mid-tier for SSO, then discovering deprovisioning is manual because SCIM is one tier up — the exact complaint pattern behind "SCIM vs SSO tax" commentary (https://www.stitchflow.com/blog/scim-vs-sso-tax-manual-provisioning, read 2026-07-29, `LIKELY`). The security consequence is that leavers keep access.

## 3. Auditability and governance

What the business gets: a durable, exportable, machine-readable record of who changed what; the ability to feed that into its own SIEM; retention long enough to satisfy its own regulator; and the ability to freeze data for litigation so that no admin — including a malicious one — can delete it.

This is the area where published limits are worst and buyer expectations have moved furthest. Two numbers to hold onto: Atlassian's audit log export gives **10,000 activities from the past 180 days** per CSV; Slack gives audit logs **on Enterprise only**. Those are the real constraints behind "we have audit logs."

The genuine differentiators are (a) *streaming* to the customer's SIEM rather than periodic CSV pulls, (b) multi-year retention as a purchasable option, and (c) immutability that survives an administrator.

**EXTENDS `lenses/governance.md:69,71,155,157,159,163,165` and `compliance.md:110,116`.** The corpus already owns: the SoD-ruleset gap as the highest-ROI governance build (`governance.md:155`, ranked `L`), amount-threshold approval routing and 3-way match and a finance SoD ruleset as finance gaps (`:69,71`), continuous control monitoring (`:157`), comms hold/retention/eDiscovery/DLP as the biggest coverage gap versus Slack and Purview (`:159`), retention-schedule plus disposition-review (`:163`), the Workday BP-framework generalisation including the free initiate/approve/view split (`:165`), and a vendor-by-vendor audit-retention comparison including Asana 90 days, n8n ≥12 months and Purview Audit Premium 1 year (`compliance.md:110`), plus n8n's SoD-by-environment (`compliance.md:116`). None of that is repeated below.

Four additions:

1. **Retention and export are priced, not just present.** `compliance.md:110` compares retention *durations*; it does not record that the duration is a purchasable tier. Salesforce's default field history is 18 months over 20 fields, and 10 years over 60 fields is the Shield add-on. Atlassian's export is capped at 10,000 rows and 180 days. Slack gates audit logs to Enterprise entirely. The buyer-facing consequence: "we have audit logs" and "we can answer an investigation that started 200 days ago" are different products at different prices.
2. **Push-streaming versus pull-API is now the procurement line.** The corpus records Slack's Audit Logs API and Asana's Audit-Log-API-to-SIEM (`compliance.md:110`, `policy.md:89`) — both pull models. GitHub Enterprise documents the vendor *pushing* to Splunk HEC / Datadog / Elastic ECS. That difference decides whether the customer's SOC sees an event in seconds or on a cron, and it is what enterprise security questionnaires now ask for by name.
3. **A sharper four-eyes primitive than approver≠requester.** The corpus's four-eyes references are the DB-level `approver_id <> requested_by` check and n8n's builder≠approver≠deployer environment split. GitHub rulesets add a third shape: *require an approval from someone other than the last person to push*. That is stricter than approver≠requester, because it defeats the case where an authorised approver quietly amends the thing after it was approved. Worth naming as its own control.
4. **Hold-versus-retention precedence, stated as an ordering rule.** `governance.md:163` and `:41` cover Purview records management and disposition review. Google Vault documents the specific conflict rule: holds take precedence over retention rules, and *deleting a hold immediately re-subjects the data to applicable retention*. That second clause is the dangerous half — releasing a hold can destroy data instantly — and it is a semantics decision any hold model has to make explicitly.

## 4. Extensibility

What the business gets: an API good enough to build on without a support ticket, events it can react to, a place to test that is not production, and a clear statement of what it may customise without becoming a software vendor itself.

The reference implementations are well-established and worth copying literally rather than reinventing:

**Idempotency (Stripe).** `Idempotency-Key` header, POST only, keys retained *at least* 24 hours, replay returns the identical status and body — including a replayed `500` — and a request that reuses a key with *different parameters* is rejected rather than silently executed. Up to 255 characters, V4 UUIDs recommended, no PII in the key.

**Rate limiting (Shopify).** Cost-based rather than request-count-based: every schema field has an integer cost, connections cost according to pagination arguments, a leaky bucket drains at a fixed rate, and the app is *refunded* the difference between requested and actual cost. Limits scale by plan (100 → 200 → 1,000 → 2,000 points/sec), a single query can never exceed 1,000 points regardless of plan, and cost telemetry ships in the response `extensions` — with a debug header for field-level breakdown. That is the humane version of rate limiting: the client can compute its own budget.

**Versioning (two credible models).** Stripe: named major releases (`Acacia`, `Dahlia`) that may break, plus monthly release trains (`2026-06-24.dahlia`) that are guaranteed backwards-compatible and safe to adopt; SDKs pin to the version current at their release. Salesforce: every version supported a *minimum of three years*, at least *one year of notice* before retirement, and retired versions return `410 GONE` rather than silently misbehaving.

**Webhook honesty (Stripe).** Documented: at-least-once delivery, **no ordering guarantee**, retries with backoff for up to 3 days in live mode, mandatory HMAC-SHA256 signature verification with a 5-minute timestamp tolerance, and manual replay for 15 days (dashboard) / 30 days (CLI). The valuable part is that the *weaknesses* are documented so integrators build idempotent, order-independent consumers.

## 5. Data ownership

What the business gets: the ability to get all of its data out, in a documented format, without asking permission — and increasingly, the ability to read it *in place* from its own warehouse without an export step at all.

The honest reading of "no lock-in" claims: export completeness is tiered, and the tier that gets the complete export is usually the top one. Slack is the clean illustration — Free/Pro export public channels only; Business+ and Enterprise Grid can export private channels and DMs, with Business+ requiring an *application* to enable it. So the same product is "fully exportable" or "partially exportable" depending on what you paid.

The architectural differentiator in 2026 is open table formats on customer-owned storage: Delta/Iceberg in the customer's own object storage means the vendor's compute is replaceable without a migration project. Snowflake adding native Iceberg support is the market conceding the point.

## 6. Multi-entity reality (a group of companies as one customer)

This is where the market genuinely splits, and where the failure mode is architectural rather than featural. The split is not a feature gap — it is a decision made once, early, about whether the legal entity is a *row-level attribute* or a *tenant boundary*, and it determines everything downstream.

**EXTENDS `policy.md:69` and `compliance.md:247-249`.** The corpus covers one dimension of this: permission *scoping* across a hierarchy — `BranchScope` over Group→법인→branch→worksite, Rippling Supergroups that recompute on transfer, Entra administrative units, n8n project scopes (`policy.md:69`) — and it records that a 그룹 obligation cascading to 계열사 is modelled by **none of the seven vendors** (`compliance.md:247-249`). Both stand. What I add is the other four dimensions a group of companies actually buys on — consolidation, per-entity configuration, intercompany transactions, and per-entity commercial terms — plus the mechanism each vendor uses, and *why* the 계열사-cascade gap the corpus found exists. It exists because most products chose the tenant boundary, and once you have chosen it, group-level anything is a reporting add-on rather than a property of the model.

Four distinct shapes exist in the market, and the mechanism matters more than the marketing:

**Shape A — one tenant/database, entity as a field on every record.** Odoo is the clearest documented mechanism: all companies live in **one database**, with a Company field on records; set it and the record is visible only to users logged into that company, **leave it empty and the record is shared across all companies**. Users get a checkbox list of companies and a selector to switch. Intercompany transactions, when enabled, *generate the counterpart document automatically* — confirm an invoice in company A and the vendor bill appears in company B, with sales orders, purchase orders/RFQs and stock moves synchronised the same way, optionally auto-validated. Workday is the same shape at enterprise scale: multiple legal entities in a single tenant, with the Company worktag hierarchy as the consolidation spine and eliminations native. NetSuite OneWorld and Sage Intacct are the finance-specific references; Rippling in workforce; Ramp in spend.

**Shape B — many accounts, one control plane. Note that control planes come in two thicknesses.** *Thick:* Stripe Organizations gives one login across accounts, org-level SSO configured once, consolidated balance/activity/payout reports, and cross-account SQL via Sigma — the group layer carries data. *Thin:* Notion Enterprise can group multiple workspaces into one organization where an org owner manages workspaces, members and settings centrally — but the workspaces remain "completely separate", content cannot be linked between them, and there is no cross-workspace search. That distinction is the one to hold onto: **an org layer that unifies administration is not an org layer that unifies data**, and vendors describe both as "multi-workspace support".

**Shape C — one org per entity, no group layer at all.** Xero: each organisation is an isolated silo with its own login and chart of accounts and no awareness the others exist; no native cross-organisation consolidation, filled by third-party add-ons (Fathom, Spotlight, Syft). QuickBooks Online is reported the same way. The tell is that Xero's own product-ideas board carries this as a long-running open request.

**Shape D — the deliberate architectural choice, made per customer.** Salesforce is the interesting case because it refuses to decide for you: single-org versus multi-org is a documented strategy question, and the guidance turns on how unified the group's *processes* are, not how many legal entities it has. Groups whose subsidiaries share processes are pushed toward one org; groups whose subsidiaries genuinely replicate a model independently are told to run separate orgs and push a managed package from a central org for local administration with standard processes. The two costs named are explicit: a single org makes per-subsidiary data governance harder, and multi-org multiplies licence cost for anyone who needs access to more than one org.

Three details that only show up after purchase:

**"Supports multi-entity" routinely means multi-entity *reporting*, not multi-entity *configuration*.** Ramp is the honest documented case: all entities in one instance with per-entity payment settings, bank accounts, subsidiary mappings and sync settings — but **spend limits cannot be delegated by entity**, underwriting is company-wide, and an employee gets one physical card across all entities. Those constraints are invisible in any feature matrix and decisive in month three.

**Per-entity pricing can invert the cost model.** In some Shape A systems each new legal entity carries a recurring fee, so a holding company with 30 SPVs pays more for entities than for users. This is the specific reason an entity-heavy group cannot simply adopt the vendor the feature matrix recommends.

**Intercompany is the hardest part and the least evenly delivered.** Odoo generates the counterpart document; Workday eliminates natively; Xero has no concept of the other entity existing. There is no middle ground worth relying on — either the model knows two entities transacted, or a human reconciles it in a spreadsheet every month.

**The design question this forces.** Whoever builds for a group of companies must decide *early* whether the entity is a first-class object in the data model or a tenant boundary. Odoo's documented mechanism is the cheap version of the right answer: an entity field on every record, plus a defined meaning for *empty* (shared across all companies). That single nullable field is what buys cross-entity reporting, per-entity configuration, delegated per-entity administration and intercompany generation — all four fall out of one modelling decision. Retrofitting it is the thing Xero has not done in years of being asked.

This also explains the corpus finding at `compliance.md:247-249` — that none of the seven benchmarked vendors model a 그룹 obligation cascading to 계열사. It is not an oversight seven times over. A cascade requires the group to be a *node in the same model* as the entities, and most products made the entity a tenant boundary, at which point the group can only ever be a reporting overlay. The vendors that could express a cascade are the ones that kept everything in one database with an entity field (Odoo's shape) or one tenant with an entity hierarchy (Workday's shape).

**Where buyers get burned, ranked by how often it appears:**
1. "Supports multiple entities" turns out to mean multi-entity *reporting* only — configuration, approvals and permissions remain global or must be duplicated N times (Ramp's per-entity spend-limit gap is the documented instance).
2. The org layer unifies administration but not data, so cross-entity search and cross-entity linking silently do not exist (Notion's documented shape).
3. Per-entity fees make the recommended vendor the most expensive one for an entity-heavy group.
4. Intercompany has no middle ground: either the counterpart document is generated, or someone reconciles it in Excel monthly, forever.

What the business gets: the ability to answer "is anyone using this, who, how much, and what is it costing us" without buying a third-party tool.

## 9. Pricing and packaging patterns

What the business gets: a price that tracks the value it receives rather than the number of people who have to touch the tool once a quarter.

The structural finding, and it is the important one for a platform that many employees touch occasionally: **pure per-seat is being abandoned by vendors, not just resented by buyers.** The credible mechanism is visible in Salesforce's Agentforce repricing, which now runs three meters in parallel: Flex Credits at $500 per 100,000 credits (≈$0.005/credit, ~20 credits per standard action ≈ $0.10, ~30 for a voice action ≈ $0.15), a $2.00-per-conversation option, and per-user licences from roughly $5 to $550/month. The stated reason for moving off $2-per-conversation was that it was unpredictable and punitive for smaller customers.

For an "everything platform" where most employees are occasional participants, the per-seat model is actively hostile: the buyer either pays for dormant seats or excludes people from the system of record. The observable industry answers are (a) tiered seat *types* — full seat vs light/contributor vs free viewer, (b) a platform fee plus consumption on the metered activity, and (c) entity/workspace-based pricing (see §6, and note the trap: per-entity fees punish exactly the group-of-companies buyer).

I could not verify the widely-repeated adoption statistics for hybrid pricing. Multiple secondary sources cite Chargebee's 2025 State of Subscriptions (43% hybrid today → 61% by end-2026) and Growth Unhinged's 2025 State of B2B Monetization (hybrid 27% → 41% in twelve months; seat-based 21% → 15%). I did not reach either primary report. Directionally consistent across sources; numerically `UNCERTAIN`.

## 10. Localisation and regional compliance — Korea specifically

What the business gets: a product that is legal to use in its jurisdiction, priced and invoiced in the local currency and format, with data where the regulator expects.

**EXTENDS `compliance.md:31,80,158,225,243-249`.** The corpus already establishes: PIPA and the Location Information Act as distinct regimes requiring scenario-specific counsel (`:31`); that no benchmarked vendor ships Korean statutory content, while Purview ships 320+ regulatory templates and Rippling maintains US employment-law content for you (`:80,158`); that seeded packs would need to be 근로기준법 / 산업안전보건법 / 개인정보보호법 / 4대보험 rather than GDPR/ACA (`:243`); that a compliance calendar would need Korean statutory dates (`:225`); the 전자결재 culture point (`:245`); and the 그룹→계열사 cascade gap (`:247-249`). All of that stands.

What the corpus does *not* contain is the **obligation content itself** — the actual clocks, thresholds, formats and market-access gates. It correctly identifies Korean content as a gap; it does not fill it. Four concrete obligations follow, with numbers. Note also one thing entirely absent from the corpus: **CSAP is being restructured**, which is a market-access gate for public-sector sales on a 2027 deadline, not a content-library question.

Korea has four distinct obligations that a platform serving Korean businesses will collide with, and they are unusually concrete compared with most jurisdictions.

**1. Privacy (PIPA).** Breach reporting is on a **72-hour** clock to the PIPC (or KISA) when a breach involves 1,000+ data subjects, sensitive/unique-identifying information, or illegal external access to systems — and data subjects must also be notified within 72 hours. Failure to notify in time carries an administrative fine up to KRW 30 million. Cross-border transfer generally needs explicit consent, with carve-outs including where the recipient has ISMS-P-grade security measures in place or the destination is recognised as adequate; the PIPC recognised the EU/EEA framework as equivalent on 2025-09-16. Critically for architecture: a transfer can be triggered by *remote access* — a team outside Korea reading Korean user data is a cross-border transfer.

**2. Tax (VAT + e-tax invoice).** VAT is 10% and applies to SaaS. Non-resident suppliers of electronic services to Korean consumers must use Simplified Business Registration (간편사업자등록, VAT Act Art. 53-2, in force since 2015-07-01), with **no turnover threshold** and registration due within 20 days of the first B2C supply; B2B is reverse-charged to the Korean buyer. Separately, Korea runs one of the world's oldest mandatory e-invoicing regimes: the 전자세금계산서 e-Tax Invoice, structured XML, PKI-signed, transmitted to the National Tax Service typically within one day of issuance — mandatory for corporations since 2011 and progressively extended to smaller businesses (individual-business threshold reported at KRW 100 million annual supply value).

**3. Public-sector cloud (CSAP → N2SF).** CSAP, the certification gating public-sector cloud sales, is being restructured: the dual Ministry of Science and ICT / National Intelligence Service verification is being consolidated under the NIS using the N2SF framework, common security requirements are moving into ISMS, guidelines are to be revised during 2026, with roughly a year's grace to full implementation in **July 2027**. Trade press advises new entrants to obtain current CSAP first to secure conversion terms.

**4. Data residency.** Achievable today from at least one mainstream vendor: Atlassian offers **South Korea** as a selectable data-residency realm, on Standard, Premium and Enterprise plans.

One practical trap worth naming: **Stripe is not available for Korea-domiciled businesses.** Stripe supports *accepting* Korean cards and wallets (Kakao Pay, Naver Pay, Samsung Pay, PAYCO) and KRW as a currency (100 KRW minimum) — but the merchant must be domiciled in one of the listed countries, and Korea is not among them. A Korean-entity platform cannot simply adopt Stripe as its merchant of record.

## Table stakes we must not ship without

Ordered by how reliably a serious buyer will block on it.

1. **SAML/OIDC SSO on a tier a mid-market company will actually buy.** Enterprise-only SSO is now read as a security-hostile pricing choice, not clever monetisation. `CONFIRMED` that this is contested publicly (ssotax.org, updated 2026-04-24) and `CONFIRMED` that at least one respected product ships it mid-tier (Notion Business, $20).
2. **SCIM provisioning.** Still an Enterprise upsell nearly everywhere — but the *absence* of it means leavers keep access, which is the specific failure buyers get burned by. Ship it; decide separately what tier.
3. **An audit log with an API, an explicit retention window, and export that is not row-capped.** The bar to beat is unimpressive (10,000 rows / 180 days at Atlassian; Enterprise-only at Slack) and beating it is cheap.
4. **A published, tiered uptime SLA with service credits** — and no pretence of a commitment on free tiers. Atlassian's 99.9% / 99.95% split with 5/10/25/50% credit bands is a directly copyable shape.
5. **A public status page with incident and maintenance history**, hosted independently of the app.
6. **SOC 2 Type II and ISO 27001, available through a self-serve trust centre.** The artefact matters less than the buyer being able to get it without a sales call.
7. **Breach notification committed in the contract at 24–48 hours to the customer**, so the customer can meet its own 72-hour statutory clock (GDPR Art. 33; Korea PIPA 72h with the 1,000-subject trigger).
8. **Idempotency keys on every mutating API call**, with Stripe's exact semantics: replay returns the identical response, key reuse with different parameters errors.
9. **Webhooks with signature verification, documented retry window, and an explicit statement that delivery is at-least-once and unordered.**
10. **Rate limits the client can compute** — cost or budget returned in the response, not just a 429.
11. **A versioning and deprecation policy with a number in it.** Salesforce's "minimum 3 years, ≥1 year notice, retired versions return 410" is the clearest published shape.
12. **Complete data export on every paid tier**, via API, in a documented format, including history. Not gated to the top tier.
13. **A non-production environment with realistic data and a supported promotion path.** Config changes tested in prod is a governance finding.
14. **Entity as a first-class object in the data model, if the customer is ever a group of companies** — with a defined meaning for the empty/unset case (Odoo: empty Company field = shared across all companies). This is the one item on the list that cannot be retrofitted cheaply; Xero is the cautionary case, and it is also the structural reason the corpus found no vendor modelling a 그룹→계열사 obligation cascade.
15. **A stated answer to hold-versus-retention precedence**, if holds exist at all. Google Vault's rule — holds win, and releasing a hold immediately re-subjects data to retention — is a semantics decision, not a feature, and it has to be made deliberately because the release path can destroy data.
16. **For Korea:** KRW handling, 10% VAT, and a path to 전자세금계산서 XML/NTS submission. These are legal obligations, not features.

## Differentiators worth choosing deliberately

Each of these is a real cost. Pick on purpose.

- **Audit log *streaming* to the customer's SIEM** (push to Splunk HEC / Datadog / Elastic ECS), not just a pollable API. Turns a compliance checkbox into a procurement advantage. Cost: an egress pipeline and per-SIEM schema mapping.
- **Multi-year audit/history retention as a purchasable option** — the Salesforce Field Audit Trail shape (10 years vs an 18-month default). Cost: storage tiering and a retention-policy model.
- **Customer-managed keys with genuine revocation.** Worth it only if you will also document the consequences as bluntly as Snowflake does (10 minutes to total operational stop). Cost: KMS integration, key-unavailability handling, and support burden when customers lock themselves out.
- **Country-level data residency** (not region-level), with an honest published list of what cannot be pinned. Korea residency is achievable today — Atlassian offers it.
- **Immutability / legal hold that survives an administrator** (Purview Preservation Lock shape). Cost: an append-only storage path and a hold model that overrides deletion everywhere, including background jobs.
- **Fine-grained, relationship-based authorization** (Zanzibar/OpenFGA-style) instead of flat roles. Buy the differentiator only if the domain is genuinely graph-shaped — cross-entity sharing, inherited permissions, per-record scoping. Otherwise RBAC plus a few attributes is the lazier correct answer.
- **Multiple pricing meters with the buyer choosing** (Agentforce shape: credits / per-transaction / per-seat), paired with **budgets, forecast alerts and hard caps** (Snowflake shape). The meter without the brake is a liability transfer.
- **Cost and adoption observability the customer can act against them with** — seat utilisation, last-login age buckets, per-object cost attribution. Costs you revenue in the short run; it is the most credible trust signal on this list precisely for that reason.
- **Delegated per-entity administration.** Genuinely differentiating for group-of-companies buyers, and the thing Shape-A vendors most commonly get wrong (see Ramp: entities are first-class for payments, but spend limits cannot be delegated by entity).
- **A thick group layer rather than a thin one.** Deciding deliberately whether the group node carries *data* (consolidated reporting, cross-entity search, cross-entity linking — Stripe Organizations' shape) or only *administration* (members, settings, billing — Notion's shape). Both are legitimate; shipping the thin one while buyers hear the thick one is where the complaint comes from. Cost of the thick version: every query needs an entity-scope decision, and cross-entity reads need their own authorization story.
- **Automatic intercompany counterpart generation.** The Odoo shape — confirm in A, the bill appears in B — is the single highest-leverage thing a multi-entity product can do, and the alternative is a permanent monthly spreadsheet. Cost: a document-pairing model, a per-pair configuration surface, and a decision about whether generated documents arrive validated or draft.
- **Templated, opinionated onboarding** (SuiteSuccess shape). The customer's real cost is decisions, not configuration.
- **ISO 42001** if AI features are central. Standard exists and is real; how often it is demanded is not established.

## Open questions

Things a plan should not assume it knows.

1. **RPO/RTO.** I found no published numeric backup/DR commitments for mainstream business SaaS. Is this genuinely never published, or did I look in the wrong place (MSAs, not trust centres)? Unresolved and material.
2. **The 2026 PIPA amendment.** The 10%-of-revenue fine ceiling, 2026-09-11 effective date, and "possibility of a breach" notification trigger come only from low-quality secondary sources. Must be checked against PIPC primary text before any Korea plan relies on it.
3. **Current Korean e-Tax Invoice thresholds.** Reported at KRW 100m for individual businesses with further reductions expected through 2026–27; confirm with NTS/홈택스 rather than tax-tech blogs.
4. **Stripe merchant availability in Korea.** My evidence is absence from Stripe's country list. Confirm directly — if wrong, it changes payments architecture significantly. If right, what do Korean-entity platforms actually use as merchant of record?
5. **Whether SCIM is crossing into mid-tier.** Both products I checked directly (Notion, Linear) keep SCIM at Enterprise. Two data points is not a trend. Worth checking 5–10 more before concluding it is safe to keep SCIM as an upsell.
6. **Native warehouse sync in business SaaS.** I could not establish that vendors ship this natively rather than leaving it to Fivetran/Hightouch. If native sync is rare, it is a differentiator; if it is becoming common, it is table stakes. Currently `UNCERTAIN`.
7. **Bring-your-own-storage for business applications.** Established in data platforms (Delta/Iceberg on customer storage), not established at all in business SaaS. Is there any real precedent?
8. **Hybrid pricing adoption numbers.** Every figure I found traces to Chargebee's or Growth Unhinged's primary reports, which I did not reach. If pricing strategy depends on the magnitude, read the primaries.
9. **Historical uptime transparency.** I confirmed Salesforce publishes live status; I did not confirm whether historical per-instance uptime percentages are public. What is the actual best-in-class for retrospective availability reporting?
10. **Guided-setup and data-import quality.** No credible sources at all. Both are consistently reported as where implementations overrun, and both are entirely unmeasured in public material.
11. **Delegated administration scoping.** Partially advanced, still open. I now have two data points — Notion's org-owner role manages workspaces centrally but the workspaces stay data-separate, and Ramp explicitly cannot delegate spend limits per entity — plus the corpus's Entra administrative units (`policy.md:69`). What I still could not find is a vendor that documents a *subsidiary admin* who can configure their own entity without global tenant rights. If that genuinely does not exist in mainstream SaaS, it is a differentiator rather than a table stake; I cannot yet tell which.
12. **Whether any vendor models a group-level obligation cascading to subsidiaries.** The corpus found none across seven vendors (`compliance.md:247-249`). My independent research did not find one either, and the architectural explanation above says why. But "two searches found nothing" is not proof of absence — the place to look would be group-audit and consolidated-ESG-reporting tools, which neither of us sampled.
13. **Workday's consolidation architecture.** The "real-time in-memory consolidation without batch delays" claim comes from vendor-adjacent secondary sources only. The *structure* (legal entities in one tenant, Company worktag hierarchy) is well-attested; the performance framing is not.
14. **Salesforce's single-vs-multi-org guidance is anchored on a 2014 primary source.** The framework is restated constantly and appears durable, but if this decision matters, find a current Salesforce architecture reference rather than relying on the dated original.
15. **Contract-level norms generally.** Indemnity caps, liability, price-uplift caps, audit rights, exit assistance: not public, and this research cannot speak to them. If those matter, the source is a procurement lawyer, not the web.
